## Overview of the Mahābhārata

The **Mahābhārata** is an ancient Indian epic that has captivated the hearts of its people for several millennia, influencing various aspects of Indian culture such as Sanskrit literature, vernacular literature, arts, crafts, painting, music, dance, drama, and temple motifs. The influence can be seen in the emotional response from simple village folks listening to ballads on Sitā’s banishment and highly skilled artisans working on temple sculptures depicting the Kurukṣetra war (Source 2).

The epic is attributed to Vyāsa Kṛṣṇadvaipāyana, also known as Vedavyāsa. Traditional lore credits him with authorship of the original work called **Jaya**, a much smaller version consisting of around 8,800 verses commemorating the victory (jaya) of the Pāṇḍava princes over their cousins, the Kauravas. Subsequently, this was revised and enlarged into **Bhārata** by Vaiśampāyana, a disciple of Vyāsa, who recited it during the Sarpayāga (serpent sacrifice) of Janamejaya (Source 2).

The final version of Mahābhārata was recited at the Sattrayāga (a kind of sacrifice performed over several years) by Suta Ugraśravas, son of Lomaharṣaṇa. This edition is known as **Śatasāhasrī Samhitā** and comprises approximately 100,000 verses, though the extant text contains fewer (Source 2).

## Structure and Content

The Mahābhārata consists of eighteen major books or parvans, each with numerous sub-parvans and chapters. Here is a brief summary:

- **Ādiparva**: Covers episodes connected with Śukrācārya, Devayānī, Yayāti, the romance of Śakuntalā and Duṣyanta, as well as stories about the ancestors and birth of Pāṇḍavas and Kauravas (Source 2).
- **Sabhāparva**: Deals with Yudhiṣṭhira's performance of the Rājasuya sacrifice and the game of dice that results in tragic consequences for the Pāṇḍavas (Source 2).
- **Araṇyaparva** (also known as Vanaparva): Covers the exile of the Pāṇḍavas, stories about Nala and Damayantī, Sāvitri and Satyavān, sages like Rṣyaśṛṅga, Agastya, and Mārkaṇḍeya, kings Bhagīratha and Sibi (Source 2).
- **Virātaparva**: Depicts the Pāṇḍavas in incognito at Virāṭa, Arjuna’s slaying of Kīcaka, and the battle to rescue cattle captured by the Kauravas (Source 2).
- **Udyogaparva**: Details peace parleys and war preparations, including the touching scene where Kuntī reveals Karṇa's birth secret and Śrī Kṛṣṇa’s final bid for peace (Source 2).
- **Bhiṣmaparva**: Contains the Bhagavadgitā and detailed descriptions of the first ten days of the war with Bhiṣma’s mortal wounding by Arjuna, who chooses to lie on a bed of arrows until the beginning of Uttarāyaṇa (Source 2).
- **Droṇaparva**: Features Droṇa's heroic exploits, Abhimanyu’s death in battle (Source 2).
- **Karṇaparva**: Describes Duśśāsana and Karṇa’s gory deaths at the hands of Bhīma and Arjuna respectively (Source 2).
- **Śalyaparva**: Narrates the final encounter between Bhima and Duryodhana, leading to the latter's demise (Source 2).
- **Sauptikaparva**: Details Aśvatthāman’s night massacre of the Pāṇḍava army (Source 2).
- **Striparva**: Describes the lamentations of the widows and women after the war (Source 2).
- **Śāntiparva** and **Ānuśāsanikaparva**: Contain discourses on dharma by Bhīṣma, including Viṣṇu sahasranāma, Śivasahasranāma, and Anugitā (Source 2).
- **Āśvamedhikaparva**: Describes Yudhiṣthira’s horse-sacrifice and the humiliation of a sage by his queen (Source 2).
- **Āśramavāsikaparva** and **Sauptikaparva**: Cover further aftermath and battles (Source 2).
- **Mausala Parva**: Details the end of Yadu dynasty (Source 2).
- **Mahāprasthānikaparva** and **Svargārohaṇikaparva**: Conclude with Yudhiṣṭhira’s journey to heaven (Source 8).

## Characters

The epic features a wide array of characters, notably:
- **Śrī Kṛṣṇa**: The divine charioteer who delivers the Bhagavadgitā.
- **Pāṇḍavas**: Yudhiṣṭhira, Bhīma, Arjuna, Nakula, and Sahadeva.
- **Kauravas**: Duryodhana and his brothers.
- **Bhīṣma** and **Droṇa**: Important warriors on the Kaurava side.

## Cultural Significance

The Mahābhārata is celebrated for its richness in meanings and Srutis, noted for its pleasantness and the glory it spreads (Source 7). It has also been utilized by various traditions for propagandistic purposes (Source 6).

## Historical Context

The epic includes narratives involving divine and demonic knowledge, such as the mṛtasanjīvinī vidya, seen in the tale of Kaca and Śukrācārya. The Mahābhārata is referenced in discussions about kṣatriya duties to uphold dharma and combat evil (Source 6).

The Harivaṃśa Purana adds details about Krishna's life and deeds, although specific episodes like the Rasa dance are not included within the main text of the Mahābhārata (Source 7).

## Conclusion

The **Mahābhārata** remains a monumental work that continues to inspire and guide people through its profound teachings on dharma, artha, kama, and moksha. It is a testament to the enduring cultural and spiritual legacy of ancient India.
