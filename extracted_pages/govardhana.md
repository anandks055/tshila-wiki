## Overview

Govardhana, meaning "that which helps in the prosperity of the cows," is a small hillock located about 25 kilometers (16 miles) from Mathurā in Uttar Pradesh. The hill is significant for its role in the Hindu myth where Kṛṣṇa lifted it to protect villagers and cattle from an unprecedented deluge caused by Indra, the king of gods.[1][4]

## Mythological Significance

When Kṛṣṇa was living in Vṛndāvana, he observed that the village elders were preparing for the annual festival dedicated to Indra. To humble Indra's pride, Kṛṣṇa advised them to worship Govardhana instead, believing it and the cows were their true protectors.[1] This suggestion was accepted by the villagers, leading to Indra’s anger and subsequent deluge. In response, Kṛṣṇa lifted the hill with one hand, providing shelter for the people beneath it. Realizing his mistake, Indra apologized and left for his heavenly abode.[1]

Kṛṣṇa's feat earned him the titles 'Govardhana-giridhārī' (one who lifted the Govardhana hill), 'Govinda' (protector of cows), and 'Upendra' (above or superior to Indra).[1] The hill is also significant in Hindu mythology, as described in texts like the Harivamsa.[5]

## Pilgrimage and Worship

Pilgrims who visit Govardhana sometimes perform a ritual known as parikrama (circumambulation) according to directions from smṛtis and purāṇas. The ceremonial worship of the hillock, called 'Govardhanapujā', is typically held on Kārttika-śukla-pratipad (the first day of the bright fortnight in the month of Kārttika, generally in November), also known as Balipratipadā.[1] This festival involves worshiping cows and bulls. In other places, a replica of the hillock is used for worship.[1]

## Historical Significance

Govardhana has been noted as a significant political center during the reigns of Nahapana and Pulumivi.[5] It also appears in historical texts such as the Yoginitantra, which mentions that the hill was made from the ashes of the demon Keśī. The Harivamsa describes it as huge with a high summit resembling Mount Mandara and featuring a large fig tree.[4]

## Other Locations

There is another area near Nāsik named Govardhana, containing numerous bathing places and pools called kuṇḍas. Surdās is also said to have lived in this vicinity after his initiation.[2] Additionally, there are references to Govardhana as a district in the Godāvarī region known for its villages along the southern road.[3]

## Literary References

Govardhana has been referenced by various authors and texts. Kalidasa mentions it in his Raghuvamsa (VI. 61), and it is also noted in works like the Subodhinī, Amṛta-sāgarī, Maitrāvaruṇaprayoga, Tattvacintā-maṇidīdhiti, Tājikapadmakośa, Nāmāvalī lex., Śrīpatipaddhati jy., Sambandhopadeśaṭīkā vaiś., Rukmiṇīcampū, Vedāntacintāmaṇi, Ghaṭakarparaṭīkā, Gaṇitasāra, Padmakośa astrol., and Līlāvatīṭīkā astron.[6]

## Grammatical Works

Govardhana is also known for his contributions to Sanskrit grammar. He authored a work on Katantra Grammar called कातन्त्रकौमुदी and wrote a commentary on the Ganaratnamahodadhi of Vardhamana.[7] A gloss on the Unadisutras is also attributed to him.

## Conclusion

Govardhana, as both a geographical feature and a mythological entity, holds significant religious and historical importance in Hindu tradition. It remains an important pilgrimage site and continues to be celebrated through various festivals and rituals.
