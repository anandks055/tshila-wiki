## Overview

Kāliya is one of the well-known Krodhavaśa serpents residing in Talātala and Mahātala, renowned for his multi-headed form. He is particularly famous for being subdued by Krishna, an event celebrated through various rituals and festivals such as Nāgapañcami, where images of him are bathed with milk [1][2][3][4].

## Mythological Context

In Hindu mythology, Kāliya appears in numerous texts including the Garuda Purana, Harivamsha Purana, Mahabharata, Vishnu Purana, Brahma Purana, Bhagavata Purana, and Skanda Purana. According to these sources, Kāliya is a venomous serpent residing in the Yamunā river, whose presence poisons the environment until he is defeated by Krishna [5][6].

### Defeat by Krishna

Kāliya's defeat by Krishna is a recurring theme across various texts:

- **Garuda Purana**: Describes Kaliya as one of the divine serpents worshipped during specific lunar phases, and recounts his defeat by Krishna, symbolizing triumph over evil [1][2][3].
- **Harivamsha Purana**: Mentions that Kāliya was destined to be defeated by Madhusudana (Krishna) and is also noted as a demon commanded by Kamsa [4][5].
- **Mahabharata**: Highlights the metaphorical use of Kaliya in Bhima's speech, emphasizing his defeat by Krishna [6][7].
- **Vishnu Purana**: Describes Kāliya as a serpent residing in the Yamunā river whose presence was toxic until he was subdued by Krishna. This act led herdsmen to view Krishna as divine and is also noted for the dance celebration that followed his defeat [8][9][10].
- **Brahma Purana**: Portrays Kaliya's activities as detrimental, making the Yamunā uninhabitable due to toxicity. Krishna’s victory over Kāliya in aquatic combat is celebrated as a heroic feat [11][12][13].
- **Bhagavata Purana**: Details Kāliya’s expulsion from the Yamunā river by Krishna, leading to the purification of its waters. This event is often referenced in songs and recollections of Krishna's protective nature [14][15][16][17].
- **Skanda Purana**: Depicts Kāliya as a Siddha who performed penance at Sarpa Tīrtha, and also mentions his role in assisting with a Yajña. He is portrayed as a cobra tamed by God [18][19][20].

## Cultural Significance

Kāliya’s defeat by Krishna is celebrated during the festival of Nāgapañcami, where his images are bathed with milk, symbolizing purification and reverence for the serpent deity [3]. The event also signifies Krishna's role as a protector against evil and his divine interventions.
