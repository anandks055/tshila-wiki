## Overview

Kāverī (also spelled Kaveri) is one of the most sacred rivers in India, originating from the Brahmagiri hills of Koḍagu (Coorg) district in Karnataka and flowing southeast through Karnataka and Tamil Nadu before merging into the Bay of Bengal near Kaveripattinam. It spans a total length of approximately 760 km (475 miles). The river's source is known as Talakāverī, situated about 39 km from Madikeri and 8 km from Bhāgamaṇḍala, where the water emerges from a small pool named Kuṇḍige. Every year during Tulāsaṅkramaṇa (when the sun enters Libra, generally in October), there is a sudden bursting forth of water at this site, attracting thousands for worship and bathing. [1]

## Mythological Significance

Kāverī is considered one of the seven most sacred rivers in Hinduism, often referred to as "Dakṣīṇagaṅgā" (the southern Ganges). In mythology, she was fostered by King Kavera, hence her name 'Kāverī.' She was also known as ‘Lopāmudrā’ and was married to the sage Agastya. According to legend, when Agastya was traveling south beyond the Vindhya mountains, he carried Kāverī in his water pot (kamaṇḍalu). A powerful wind upset the kamaṇḍalu near the Sahyādri mountain, causing her to flow as a river. [1]

## Pilgrimage Sites and Temples

The banks of Kāverī are dotted with several important pilgrimage centers and temples:

- **Srīraṅgam** in Tamil Nadu
- **Srīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa** near Mysore, Karnataka 
- **Madhyaraṅga temple** near Shivana samudra, Karnataka

All these sites feature temples dedicated to Raṅganātha (an aspect of Viṣṇu). The famous Śaiva temples on its banks include those at Cidambaram and Tañjāvur (Bṛhadīśvara Mahādeva). Additionally, the Jambukeśvara temple near Srīraṅgam is also very significant. [1]

## Historical and Geographical Importance

Kāveri plays a crucial role in South Indian history and geography, watering regions such as Mysore, Coorg (Koḍagu), Coimbatore, and Trichinopoly before merging into the Bay of Bengal. It is noted for its historical significance, mentioned in ancient texts like the Ramayana, Mahabharata, and Yoginitantra. The river is also associated with various dynasties such as the Pallavas and Colas, and it has been a focal point for numerous battles and conquests. [5]

## Modern Day

Today, Kāveri remains an essential source of water and agricultural sustenance in South India. It continues to be revered by millions who gather annually at its sacred sites for worship and purification rituals.
