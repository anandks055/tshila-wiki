## Nakula: Overview and Characteristics

Nakula, one of the twin sons of King Pāṇḍu and Queen Mādrī by the grace of the twin deities, the Aśvins (Ashwini Kumaras), is considered the fourth among the five Pandava brothers. He is renowned for his exceptional beauty and various skills, including horse management and archery.[1][2] Nakula also possesses notable administrative abilities and played significant roles in key events such as the Rajasuya sacrifice and the Kurukṣetra war.

## Family Background

Nakula was born to Mādrī through the favor of the Aśvins, making him a twin brother to Sahadeva.[1] He is the father of Śatānīka by Draupadī, the common wife of all five Pandavas, and Niramitra by Reṇumati. Both sons play important roles in subsequent events.

## Administrative and Military Roles

Before Yudhiṣṭhira began the Rājasūya sacrifice, Nakula was dispatched to collect sufficient wealth from northern countries.[2] During their exile, he assumed the name Dāmagranthi (keeper of horses) while serving King Virāṭa in his stables and managing royal horses. He excelled in horse care and training during this period.[3][16-19]

In the Kurukṣetra war, Nakula demonstrated exceptional valor and skill. He defeated Śakuni on the 14th day and Duryodhana on the 15th day of the battle but was later defeated by Karṇa.[2] Throughout the conflict, he continued to fight valiantly alongside his brothers and supported key figures like Bhima and Abhimanyu. [8-10]

## Personal Qualities

Nakula is noted for his humility and kindness, treating everyone with respect and contributing significantly to strategic decisions made by the Pandavas.[11][12] His role in important events such as the assembly preparations for war and discussions of peace underscored his diplomatic and military capabilities. [13][14]

## Tragic End

During their final journey to heaven (mahāprasthāna), Nakula was the third Pandava brother to fall, attributed by Yudhiṣṭhira to his pride in his handsome appearance.[2] Additionally, during a nocturnal attack led by Ashvatthama, his son Śatānīka met a tragic end. [18]
