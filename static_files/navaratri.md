Navaratri is one of the most significant and widely celebrated festivals in Hindu tradition, marking nine nights dedicated to the worship of the divine feminine or Shakti [ref1]. This festival, observed across India with diverse rituals and customs, embodies the richness of Indic knowledge systems and heritage by integrating various elements such as mythology, art, and cultural practices. The celebration involves elaborate ceremonies and offerings to different manifestations of the goddess, culminating in Vijayadashami on the tenth day [ref1]. Each night is associated with a specific form of the divine mother, symbolizing virtues ranging from wisdom to strength, thus underscoring the festival's profound spiritual significance within Hindu culture. The Navaratri step-doll arrangements represent one such manifestation, where marappācī dolls embody the eight manifestations of Mahalakṣmī [ref11]. These dolls are not just artistic expressions but also encapsulate deeper philosophical and cultural meanings inherent in Indic knowledge systems.

## Origin and Mythology

The origins of Navaratri can be traced back to ancient Hindu scriptures, where various mythological narratives offer explanations for the festival's inception. One prevalent story revolves around the battle between Goddess Durga and the demon Mahishasura [ref1]. According to legend, the gods sought help from the divine feminine to defeat the powerful demon who had gained immunity against all male warriors due to a boon. The goddess Durga, armed with weapons gifted by various deities, engaged in a fierce nine-day battle that culminated in her victory over Mahishasura on the tenth day, known as Vijayadashami [ref1].

Another mythological narrative associated with Navaratri is linked to the birth of Goddess Sarasvati and the significance of the Sarasvati river. In some versions, the festival celebrates the emergence of Sarasvati from the waters of the sacred river, emphasizing her role as the goddess of knowledge, music, arts, and wisdom [ref8]. The ancient Rigveda mentions Sarasvati as a prominent river and symbolizes it as a source of spiritual and intellectual nourishment [ref8].

## Regional Variations

Navaratri celebrations vary significantly across different regions in India, each highlighting unique customs, rituals, and cultural practices. In North India, the festival is marked by elaborate puja ceremonies dedicated to Goddess Durga, followed by garba and dandiya dances, which are vibrant community events involving music and dance [ref1]. The nine nights of Navaratri also correspond to the worship of different forms of the goddess, with each day featuring specific rituals and offerings tailored to honor a particular deity.

In South India, the festival is known as Bommai Kolu or Golu, where women arrange step-dolls (Bommais) on an elaborate platform called "Kolu" [ref1]. This tradition involves showcasing dolls in various sizes and designs that represent different aspects of Indian culture and mythology. The arrangement of these dolls on nine steps symbolizes the victory of good over evil and is often accompanied by special prayers, offerings, and feasts shared among family members and friends.

In West Bengal, Navaratri assumes a particularly significant form as Durga Puja, which is celebrated with grand processions, vibrant pandals (temporary structures), and elaborate idols of Goddess Durga [ref1]. The festival not only marks the victory of Durga over Mahishasura but also celebrates the return of the goddess to her natal home. Bengalis prepare for this event months in advance by building intricate idols and designing thematic pandals that attract visitors from all over India.

## Rituals and Practices

The rituals and practices associated with Navaratri are extensive and varied, reflecting the diverse cultural tapestry of India. Each day of the festival is dedicated to a specific form or manifestation of the goddess, with unique customs and offerings made accordingly [ref1]. For instance, on the first three days, worshippers focus on Durga as the warrior goddess, invoking her power to vanquish evil forces in their lives. Devotees often fast during these days, offering prayers and performing special pujas (worship ceremonies) that include lighting lamps, chanting mantras, and reciting hymns.

The middle three days are dedicated to Lakshmi, the goddess of wealth and prosperity [ref1]. During this period, devotees perform rituals aimed at attracting abundance and financial success. Homes are cleaned thoroughly, and elaborate rangolis (colorful designs made on floors) are created using colored powders or flowers. Special offerings are made to Lakshmi in hopes that she will bestow her blessings upon the household.

The final three days honor Sarasvati as the goddess of knowledge and wisdom [ref8]. Scholars, students, and artists pay special homage to Sarasvati by laying their books, musical instruments, and creative tools at her feet. This act symbolizes seeking divine guidance and inspiration in one's intellectual pursuits.

## Cultural Significance

Navaratri holds immense cultural significance across India, transcending religious boundaries and fostering a sense of community among its practitioners [ref1]. The festival serves as an important social event where people gather to celebrate and reaffirm their shared values and traditions. Through the rituals and practices observed during Navaratri, individuals express gratitude for the blessings bestowed upon them by the divine feminine.

Moreover, Navaratri provides a platform for showcasing and preserving traditional arts and crafts [ref1]. The elaborate step-doll arrangements in South India or the vibrant pandals in West Bengal exemplify the rich artistic heritage of these regions. These displays not only attract tourists but also inspire local artisans to continue their craft, ensuring that these traditions are passed on to future generations.

## Historical Context

The historical context of Navaratri can be traced back through various inscriptions and texts that document its evolution over time [ref2]. For instance, the copper-plate grant of King Narendra-dhavala found in Madras Museum provides valuable insights into the significance of religious festivals during medieval times [ref2]. This inscription mentions the revival of the Velvikudi grant by Neduňjadaiyan, highlighting the importance of such events for both religious and administrative purposes.

Additionally, the role of historical figures like Bhoja, an imperial Pratīhāra ruler whose inscription is found in Gwalior, underscores the significance of Navaratri in ancient Indian history [ref4]. The texts and inscriptions from these periods offer a glimpse into how different dynasties integrated religious festivals into their cultural and administrative frameworks.

## Contemporary Celebrations

Contemporary celebrations of Navaratri reflect both tradition and innovation, blending age-old customs with modern practices. While many continue to adhere strictly to traditional rituals, others have adapted the festival to fit contemporary lifestyles [ref1]. For example, in urban areas where space constraints make elaborate step-doll arrangements impractical, families often opt for smaller, more portable displays that can be set up indoors.

Moreover, technological advancements have transformed how Navaratri is celebrated today. Digital platforms now allow people to participate in virtual puja sessions and watch live streams of traditional dances from their homes [ref1]. Social media has also played a significant role in spreading awareness about the festival's significance and sharing innovative ways to celebrate it, thereby fostering a sense of global community among Hindus.

## Philosophical Underpinnings

The philosophical underpinnings of Navaratri are deeply rooted in Indic knowledge systems and reflect core principles such as dharma (righteousness) and sṛṣṭi (creation) [ref1]. The festival symbolizes the triumph of good over evil, a fundamental theme in Hindu philosophy. This concept is embodied through the various manifestations of the divine feminine, each representing different virtues and attributes.

Furthermore, Navaratri serves as an opportunity for self-reflection and spiritual growth. By engaging in fasting, prayer, and other devotional practices, individuals seek to purify their minds and souls [ref1]. The festival also emphasizes the importance of knowledge and wisdom, particularly through the worship of Sarasvati on the final days.

## Artistic Expressions

Artistic expressions during Navaratri are multifaceted, encompassing a wide range of traditional crafts and performances [ref1]. Step-doll arrangements in South India are one such form that showcases intricate craftsmanship and artistic creativity. These dolls, often depicting mythological scenes or everyday life, require meticulous attention to detail.

In addition to visual arts, Navaratri also celebrates various performing arts, including music, dance, and theater. Garba and dandiya dances in Gujarat, for instance, are vibrant expressions of community spirit and cultural heritage [ref1]. These performances involve intricate choreography and traditional costumes, making them visually captivating events.

## Modern Adaptations

Modern adaptations of Navaratri reflect the evolving nature of Hindu traditions in contemporary society [ref1]. As urbanization and globalization continue to shape lifestyles, people have found innovative ways to celebrate the festival while maintaining its core spiritual essence. For example, corporate organizations often organize cultural programs that feature traditional dances and music performances during this period.

Moreover, there has been a growing trend toward eco-friendly celebrations, with many opting for sustainable practices such as using natural materials in step-doll arrangements or conducting digital pujas to reduce environmental impact [ref1]. These adaptations demonstrate how modern Hindus are balancing tradition with contemporary concerns while preserving the festival's cultural significance.
