Arishta is a multifaceted term in Indian heritage and culture that encompasses various meanings, from a spirituous liquor used in Ayurvedic medicine to a demon who was slain by the divine figure Krishna. The word Arishta can denote several senses including “unhurt,” an ill-omen foreboding misfortune or death, a medicinal tonic, and even the name of a sage who revived his son from death [ref1]. In the Harivamsha Purana, Ariṣṭa is depicted as a Danava moving around in the battlefield on a chariot drawn by twelve horses and later reincarnated as the Asura Kakudmi. The term Arishta also appears in various ancient texts such as the Sushruta Samhita where it describes medicinal preparations used for treating specific ailments, and in historical chronicles like the Sukrita-samkīrtana of Gujarāt, which provides important local historical information [ref9]. Through these diverse contexts, Arishta emerges as a rich symbol interwoven into the fabric of Indian mythology, medicine, and history [ref1].

## Etymology and Linguistic Interpretations

The term *Arishta* (अरिष्ट) is derived from Sanskrit roots that denote "unhurt" or "untouched by harm," reflecting a protective quality. In various literary contexts, it can symbolize immunity against misfortune or injury [ref1]. This etymological background underscores the term’s significance in both spiritual and medicinal practices. For instance, in Ayurveda, *Arishta* refers to specific types of decoctions prepared from grapes or certain roots used as tonics for health and well-being [ref4].

The word *Arishta* also has diverse applications, such as referring to a crow, soapberry tree (Sapindus), garlic, and even portentous phenomena observed by yogis that predict death [ref2]. These varied meanings suggest the term’s adaptability in different cultural contexts. In ancient texts like the Sushruta Samhita, *Arishta* is listed among emetic drugs, used for purgative treatments, and as an efficacious liquor that subdues deranged bodily humours [ref4].

In addition to these interpretations, *Arishta* has been recorded in various historical chronicles and Puranas. For example, the Sukrita-samkīrtana, a local chronicle of Gujarat authored by Arisima, provides valuable insights into regional history through its detailed accounts [ref9]. This multifaceted usage highlights how deeply entrenched *Arishta* is within Indian culture and tradition.

## Mythological Contexts

In Hindu mythology, the term Arishta appears in multiple narratives where it signifies a demon or entity that poses a threat to divine order. One of the most prominent stories involves Arishta as a Danava who attacked young Krishna in the form of a bull [ref5]. According to the Harivamsha Purana, Ariṣṭa was one of the demons who moved around in the battlefield on a chariot drawn by twelve horses and surrounded by Danava formations. In another narrative, he is reborn as Asura Kakudmi after being killed by Krishna, adopting the form of a bull again to terrorize the cows [ref5].

The demon Arishta also features prominently in other Puranic texts such as the Brahma Purana where he exemplifies the recurring motif of cosmic conflict between divinity embodied in Kṛṣṇa and malevolent entities seeking dominance over creation. Ariṣṭa is mentioned as an influential Asura troubling humanity along with figures like Dhenuka, Keśin, and Pralamba [ref7]. The stories often describe his downfall at the hands of Krishna, highlighting themes of divine intervention and restoration of cosmic order.

In another mythological context, Arishta appears in the Vishnu Purana as one of thirteen daughters of Kashyapa. This version portrays her not just as a demon but also as an Asura who was slain by Kṛṣṇa [ref8]. These varied depictions underscore the complex role that Arishta plays within Hindu mythology, embodying both chaos and eventual resolution through divine intervention.

## Medical Applications in Ayurveda

In the context of traditional Indian medicine, particularly Ayurveda, *Arishta* has significant applications as a medicinal preparation. The term is used to denote specific types of decoctions prepared from grapes or certain roots that are utilized as tonics for promoting health and well-being [ref1]. These preparations are often prescribed based on the dosha involved in a patient's condition, reflecting Ayurveda’s holistic approach to treatment.

For instance, *Arishta* is listed among emetic drugs used in purgative treatments, especially for Kapha disorders. It is also described as a highly efficacious type of fermented liquor that tends to subdue deranged humours and serves as an excellent appetizer [ref4]. This indicates its role not only in treating specific ailments but also in maintaining overall health.

In addition to these medicinal uses, *Arishta* is mentioned in several Ayurvedic recipes. It appears in the preparation of Karanjadya Ghrita, a medicated ghee used for curing malignant ulcers and sinus [ref4]. Furthermore, it is listed among the emetic drugs and purgative treatments, highlighting its versatility as a medicinal ingredient. The use of *Arishta* in Ayurvedic formulations underscores its importance within traditional Indian medical practices.
