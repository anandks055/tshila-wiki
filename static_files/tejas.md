## Introduction to Tejas in Indian Heritage

Tejas, meaning "radiance" or "brilliance," is a concept deeply embedded within the cultural and spiritual fabric of India. It encompasses both the luminous essence attributed to deities and the radiant qualities that individuals strive to achieve through various practices and disciplines [ref1]. This term has been applied across different realms, including religious texts, philosophical treatises, and historical narratives, reflecting its profound significance in Indian heritage. The concept of Tejas is not only confined to spiritual or metaphysical contexts but also extends into cultural expressions such as art, architecture, literature, and even scientific endeavors [ref2]. This article explores the multifaceted nature of Tejas within Indian traditions, highlighting its role in sacred sites, historical periods, and scholarly institutions dedicated to preserving Indic knowledge.

## Tejas in Religious Architecture

One of the most tangible manifestations of Tejas is seen in the architecture and sculpture found at significant religious sites across India. The Dilwara temples, particularly those at Mount Abu, exemplify this concept through their intricate carvings and architectural brilliance [ref3]. Among these temples, Vimala and Tejahpāla are notable for their exquisite craftsmanship and artistic mastery [ref4], representing the radiant qualities of Tejas in physical form. These temples not only serve as places of worship but also stand as testaments to the cultural and spiritual achievements of medieval India [ref5].

The Tejahpāla temple, alongside Vimala, is renowned for its fine examples of Jain sculpture, showcasing a blend of religious devotion and artistic excellence that epitomizes the concept of Tejas. Both temples are recognized as important exemplars of Jain figure-sculpture from the medieval period, emphasizing the luminous essence attributed to spiritual figures [ref6]. The meticulous carving and severe simplicity seen in these structures reflect an aesthetic ideal rooted in the pursuit of radiance and clarity, essential attributes associated with Tejas.

## Historical Contexts of Tejas

The concept of Tejas is also deeply intertwined with various historical contexts within Indian civilization. In the Vedic period, Tejas represented a divine luminosity associated with deities such as Agni (the god of fire) [ref7]. This notion extended beyond the purely religious sphere to encompass cultural and societal values, influencing practices and beliefs across different regions and dynasties.

For instance, in the Age of Imperial Kanauj, Tejas was embodied in the political and administrative ideals upheld by ruling dynasties. The Kalachuri dynasty, though not explicitly defined, is referenced alongside other ruling families that contributed to the rich tapestry of Indian history [ref8]. These dynasties often associated themselves with Tejas through their patronage of art, architecture, and religious practices, thereby linking their reigns with divine radiance and spiritual brilliance.

## Tejas in Philosophical Traditions

The concept of Tejas is also prevalent in the philosophical traditions of India, particularly within texts like the Mahābhārata [ref9]. This epic narrative not only serves as a repository of historical information but also delves into profound philosophical and ethical teachings. The term Tejas appears frequently in various contexts within the text, symbolizing inner radiance or spiritual illumination [ref10].

In the broader context of Indic philosophy, Tejas is often equated with the concept of prana (life force) or chitta (consciousness), emphasizing its role as an intrinsic aspect of human existence and cosmic order. The itihāsas, which include works like the Mahābhārata and Rāmāyaṇa, attest to the state of samādhi—a meditative state where one achieves a profound sense of inner radiance—and highlight its reproducibility across time [ref11]. This connection between Tejas and spiritual practices underscores its significance in achieving higher states of consciousness and enlightenment.

## Geographical and Cultural Unity

Tejas plays a vital role in fostering a sense of unity among diverse regions within India. The country's vast geography, stretching over 2,000 miles from north to south and more than 1,900 miles from east to west [ref12], is seen as a single, unified entity characterized by natural boundaries such as seas and mountains. This geographical unity is complemented by cultural elements that foster shared identity among its people, including sacred sites, pilgrimage traditions, and the concept of India as a "mother country" and "heaven on earth" [ref13].

Sacred places like Sārnāth, associated with the life of the Buddha and significant for both Buddhist and Jain pilgrims, exemplify this unity. The discovery of medieval reliefs depicting religious themes at these sites underscores the enduring spiritual significance attributed to Tejas across different traditions [ref14]. Such locations serve as focal points that bring together diverse communities under a common spiritual canopy.

## Institutional Preservation of Indic Knowledge

The concept of Tejas extends into institutional efforts aimed at preserving and studying Indic knowledge systems. Oriental research institutes play a crucial role in this endeavor, serving as centers dedicated to the study of culture in all its aspects [ref15]. These institutions have been instrumental in collecting, collating, editing, and publishing rare manuscripts and texts dealing with religion, philosophy, literature, grammar, arts, and sciences.

The Adyar Library and Research Center in Chennai stands out for its extensive collection of printed books and manuscripts spanning various languages. This institution has published numerous works on Indological subjects and continues to provide resources and support for scholars engaged in research [ref16]. Similarly, the Asiatic Society in Kolkata was established by Sir William Jones with the aim of stimulating inquiries into Asian cultures and preserving valuable knowledge [ref17].

These institutions not only preserve ancient texts but also contribute significantly to ongoing scholarly discourse. The publication of critical editions of seminal works like the Mahābhārata and the preparation of detailed catalogs of manuscripts are among their key achievements [ref18]. By facilitating access to these resources, they ensure that Tejas—a concept central to Indian heritage—continues to illuminate contemporary understanding.

## Tejas in Vedic Traditions

The roots of Tejas can be traced back to the Vedic period, where it was associated with divine luminosity and cosmic order. The Vedas, comprising the Rigveda, Yajurveda, Samaveda, and Atharvaveda, are ancient texts that form the foundation of Hinduism [ref19]. Within these texts, Tejas is often invoked in hymns dedicated to Agni (fire) and other deities, symbolizing their radiant essence.

The Vājasaneyi Samhitā, a text of the White Yajurveda, includes chapters on ritual practices such as Purushamedha, which provide insights into social and economic conditions during the Vedic period [ref20]. The use of Soma plant and its substitutes in rituals further emphasizes the sacred nature of Tejas and its role in religious ceremonies.

## Regional Significance

Tejas has also played a significant role in various regions across India. Mathurā, for instance, is one of the seven sacred cities in Hinduism and holds considerable historical and cultural importance [ref21]. Its association with the Yādava dynasty and early Buddhist monuments highlights its multifaceted significance within Indian civilization.

Koppana is another region that exemplifies the broader influence of Tejas. After the decline of Jain influence in Tamil lands, Koppana became a significant site for religious iconography and temple construction [ref22]. The creation of religious structures and sculptures at this location underscores its importance in preserving and promoting the concept of Tejas through artistic expression.

## Historical Dynasties and Rulers

The concept of Tejas is also linked to historical dynasties and rulers who sought to embody or associate themselves with divine radiance. For example, Gwalior is a significant site associated with Bhoja, an imperial Pratīhāra ruler whose inscription provides valuable information about the dynasty's history [ref23]. The archaeological significance of this site further emphasizes its role in understanding ancient Indian culture and governance.

Similarly, the Kalachuri dynasty, though not explicitly defined, is mentioned alongside other ruling families that contributed to India's rich historical tapestry. These dynasties often patronized art, architecture, and religious practices, linking their reigns with Tejas through divine luminosity and spiritual brilliance [ref24].

## Conclusion

Tejas, as a concept deeply rooted in Indian heritage, encompasses both the divine radiance attributed to deities and the radiant qualities individuals strive to achieve. This term appears across various realms including religious texts, historical narratives, and scholarly institutions dedicated to preserving Indic knowledge systems.

From the intricate carvings of Dilwara temples to the philosophical teachings of epics like the Mahābhārata, Tejas symbolizes inner radiance and spiritual illumination. Its significance extends into geographical unity, fostering a shared identity among diverse regions within India. The preservation efforts by Oriental research institutes ensure that this concept continues to illuminate contemporary understanding.

Tejas thus remains an enduring aspect of Indian heritage, reflecting the country's rich cultural and spiritual legacy [ref1].
