## Parikṣit: A Devoted King and Listener of Spiritual Teachings

Parīkṣit was a significant figure in the Kuru dynasty, known for his devotion to Lord Viṣṇu and deep spiritual understanding. He was the son of prince Abhimanyu and Uttarā, granddaughter of Arjuna and Subhadrā.[1] After the Mahābhārata war, Aśvatthāma attempted to destroy the Pāṇḍava lineage by discharging a Brahmāstra towards Uttarā's womb. Despite this attack, Viṣṇu revived Parīkṣit, who was then born as a baby with divine vision, examining every corner through his eyes.[1]

Parīkṣit is also known for his devotion and spiritual insights. He approached Sage Śukabrahma seeking detailed narrations of Lord Kṛṣṇa's stories due to his profound devotion.[8] As a king, he was noted for his virtuous rule and performed numerous sacrifices that were compared to those of famous kings and deities.[4]

Parīkṣit is described as an ideal listener who undertook prāyopaveśa (giving up the body by fasting) after being instructed in the science of bhakti. He questioned Śukamaharṣi about various spiritual matters, including the story of Dakṣayajña and the encounter with a bull symbolizing dharma and satya.[5] His rule was marked by his dedication to protecting his subjects' welfare while renouncing worldly attachments in pursuit of spiritual knowledge.[7]

Parīkṣit's tragic end came when he showed disrespect to Sage Samīka, who was meditating deeply. This act led to a curse that resulted in Parīkṣit dying from the bite of Takṣaka, the king of snakes.[1] During his final days, he listened to the recitation of the Bhāgavata by Śuka, a significant spiritual text.[1]

### Lineage and Offspring

Parīkṣit was the father of Janamejaya and had three other sons: Srutasena, Ugrasena, and Bhīmasena.[1][7] He is also noted for initiating the idea of a horse-sacrifice before his death.[3]

### Spiritual Teachings and Narratives

Parīkṣit was known for his deep spiritual inquiries. He requested Sage Śukabrahma to narrate various stories, including those of Ajāmiḷa, Prahlāda, Aṁbarīṣa, Dakṣayajña, Priyavrata, and the duties of Manus.[2][5][7] His interactions with Śuka highlight his keen interest in understanding spiritual truths and the deeds of Lord Viṣṇu and Kṛṣṇa.[7]
