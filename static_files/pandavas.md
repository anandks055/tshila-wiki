## Pāṇḍavas

The Pāṇḍavas are the five brothers Yudhiṣṭhira, Bhīma, Arjuna, Nakula, and Sahadeva, who were sons of King Pāṇḍu and his two wives Kuntī and Mādrī [1][40]. They were educated by Kṛpācārya, Droṇācārya, and Bhīṣma [36], and are central figures in the mahābhārata epic. Known for their noble qualities and devotion to their mother, they faced numerous trials throughout their lives, including being tricked into living in a combustible palace in Vāraṇāvata by Duryodhana and Śakuni [28][6]. They were exiled to the forest for thirteen years during which they visited significant places such as Kailāsa, Gandhamadana parvata, Gaṅgāsāgara, and Sālihotra’s āśrama [19][16][18][31].

The Pāṇḍavas sought guidance from various sages and deities like Vidura, Maitreya, Paraśurāma, and Bhīṣma on how to seek reconciliation or peace with their adversaries [3][30][32][39]. They played a pivotal role in the Kurukṣetra war, fighting alongside Devāpi and Śikhaṇḍin against the Kauravas led by Duryodhana [5][38], and were assisted by Ghatotkaca [17].

After winning the war, they performed the Rājasuya sacrifice in their rājadhānī, Indraprastha [34]. They also undertook a final journey described in the Mahāprasthānika-parva of the mahābhārata, traveling towards heaven [30].

Their story includes visits to sacred sites like Prabhāsa and their role in building the Śiva temple in Kedāranātha to atone for sins committed during the Kurukṣetra war [35][28]. They are also known for their spiritual quests and connections to vaidika lore, as seen in the teachings of Anugitā and Viṣṇusahasranāma [4][39].

According to the Shiva Purana, they benefit from Draupadī's inexhaustible cloth, bringing them happiness [48]. They are also depicted as warriors who were defeated by Aśvatthāman’s strength, necessitating Kṛṣṇa’s intervention [49]. They sought guidance from Kṛṣṇa and Vyāsa on how to propitiate Śiva [50], and encountered Śiva in the form of a buffalo, worshipping him and receiving relief from their miseries [52].

In the Markandeya Purana, they are described as one of the warring factions alongside the Kauravas in a battle where Tārkṣī was wounded and gave birth to four eggs [54]. They are also noted as five partial incarnations of Indra, born to ease the burden of the Earth and restore Indra’s former glory [55].

The Pāṇḍavas are celebrated for their roles in various events such as celebrating marriages, engaging in battles, performing pilgrimages, and installing sacred symbols like Liṅgas according to the Skanda Purana [57][58][59][60][61][62][63][64][65][66][67][68][69][70][71][72][73][74][75][76].

In the Devi Bhagavata Purana, they are portrayed both as performers of significant rituals and figures whose actions raise questions about their moral and religious integrity. They conducted the Rājasūya Yajña but encountered subsequent misfortunes, suggesting possible errors in the performance of their ritual [78]. It is also noted that the Pāṇḍavas engaged in sacrifices driven by arrogance, thereby casting doubt on the purity of their intentions and their standing as paragons of religious conduct [79].

They are described as righteous and virtuous yet face numerous difficulties and suffer hardships throughout their lives. They are known for marrying Draupadī and are referenced in contexts involving significant events such as returning to Hastināpur after an unspecified period, the burning of Dhritarāstra and others [84][85].
