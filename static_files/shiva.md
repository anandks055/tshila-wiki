## Shiva

Śiva literally means 'the auspicious one’ and is a major deity of Indic knowledge traditions. He is the last deity of the Trinity, responsible for the dissolution of the universe [1][2]. Śiva embodies Tamas, the centrifugal inertia leading to dispersion and annihilation [1]. After destruction, all existence thins out into a boundless void, which serves as the substratum from which the universe repeatedly springs. This cycle is an inviolable law, and Śiva facilitates both disintegration and creation [1].

### Aspects of Śiva
Śiva is often referred to as Rudra in his terrific aspect; however, scholars debate whether these two deities are identical or if they represent separate entities that have been fused over time due to cultural integration. Some scholars argue that Śiva is a non-Aryan deity and more ancient than the Vedic Rudra [1].

### Portraiture of Śiva
Śiva is typically depicted as a handsome youth, white as camphor, with ash-smeared limbs that are strong and smooth. He has three eyes; his third eye being located between the eyebrows on his forehead, and four arms. Two hands hold the trident (triśula) and drum (ḍamaru), while the other two are in the abhaya and varada mudrās [1][3].

Śiva is adorned with a crown of long matted hair from which flows the river Gaṅgā, and he wears the crescent moon as a diadem. His garments consist of a tiger-skin and an elephant-skin, and his body is bedecked with serpents forming necklaces, girdles, yajñopavīta, arm-bracelets, and a garland of skulls around his blue neck [1].

### Personnel of Śiva
Pārvatī is Śiva's consort. Gaṇeśa and Kumāra are his sons. He has a large retinue including Nandi (his bull vehicle), Bhṛñgi the ṛṣi with three legs and three arms, the mouse of Gaṇeśa, the peacock of Kumāra, and numerous ghosts, goblins, and imps [1].

### Famous Stories about Śiva
Śiva's abode is in the icy Himalayas, but he often roams the earth, particularly burial grounds and cremation sites. His stories are recounted in mythological literature:

- When Pārvatī closed his two eyes playfully, darkness engulfed the world until Śiva willed a third eye to restore light.
- During the descent of Gaṅgā from heaven, she fell ferociously on Śiva’s head and was locked there by him. Only after much supplication did he allow her to stream out.
- When the ocean of milk was churned, Śiva seized the cool crescent moon as his diadem and drank the deadly poison Hālāhala to save the worlds, with Pārvatī pressing his throat to prevent it from going down.
- In Dārukavana, enraged ṛṣis tried to kill Śiva through sorcery. Out of the sacrificial fire emerged a tiger, deer, and red-hot iron; Śiva killed the tiger and wore its skin, caught hold of the deer, which he still holds in his left hand, and made the iron one of his weapons.
- Other stories include destroying Dakṣa’s sacrifice, cutting off Brahmā's fifth head for disrespect, vanquishing Tripurāsura, killing Gajāsura and wearing its hide, granting Arjuna the Pāśupatāstra weapon, appearing as a pillar of fire to teach Brahmā and Viṣṇu, saving Mārkaṇḍeya from Yama [1][40].

### Symbology
Śiva's white color symbolizes light that dispels darkness and knowledge that dispels ignorance. His three eyes represent the sun, moon, and fire—sources of light, life, and heat; they also indicate omniscience.

The river Gaṅgā on his head represents spiritual purification and bliss. The crescent moon signifies time since days and months are measured by its waxing and waning. Coiled cobras symbolize death but also cycles of time and basic energy in the microcosm, showing Śiva as the master of time and energy [1].

### Iconographic Representation
Śiva may have two to thirty-two hands holding various objects such as:
- Triśula (trident)
- Cakra (discus)
- Paraśu (battle axe)
- Damaru (drum)
- Akṣamālā (rosary)
- Mṛga (deer)
- Pāśa (noose)
- Daṇḍa (staff)
- Pināka or ajagava (bow)
- Khatvāṅga (magic wand)
- Pāśupata (spear)
- Padma (lotus)
- Kapāla (skull-cup)
- Darpaṇa (mirror)
- Khaḍga (sword)

These objects symbolize his power, mastery over spiritual sciences, and occult knowledge [1][6].

### Epilogue
Śiva is never worshipped as the mulamurti but always as an utsavamurti [1].
