## Panchavati

Panchavati, also known as Pañcavaṭī, is a significant pilgrimage site located on the banks of the Godāvari River. The name translates to "a grove of five banyan trees" [4], which are believed to have been transformed from Gandharva youths encircled by Sage Agastya as a punishment for obstructing his path [6].

### Historical and Mythological Significance

Panchavati is renowned in Hindu mythology, particularly due to its association with the epic Ramayana. According to this narrative, Rama, Sita, and Lakshmana chose Panchavati as their residence during their exile on the advice of Sage Agastya [3]. The area was known for its abundant resources and natural beauty, including a woodland setting frequented by wild beasts and deer, with flowering trees and a nearby pool where they constructed their hermitage [1].

The site is also significant in the Ramayana as it is where Sita was abducted by Ravana, leading to the epic battle of Lanka [2]. The area has been known by different names throughout various ages and has held spiritual importance for many centuries. Śri Rāmakṛṣṇa renovated Panchavati for spiritual practices, emphasizing its sacred value [1].

### Geographical Context

Panchavati is located in the vicinity of Nasik on the banks of the Godāvari River. The Chaitya cave at Nasik, which dates back to the second and third centuries CE according to Fergusson’s estimation, adds historical depth to this location [2]. The site is described as a tīrtha (sacred place) in various texts, further emphasizing its religious significance [7].

### Construction of the Hermitage

The story of Panchavati includes the building of an āśrama by Rama and Lakshmana. Initially, when they began to construct their hermitage at the center of the circle formed by five banyan trees, they encountered a demon youth named Śambhukumāra, who had transformed into one of the banyans due to his lust for Sita [6]. This incident underscores the spiritual and mystical aspects associated with Panchavati.

### Spiritual Practices

The site was renovated by Śri Rāmakṛṣṇa for spiritual practices, highlighting its enduring significance in Hindu traditions. The presence of five specific types of trees—banyan (वट), peepal (अश्वत्थ), bael (विल्व), dhatri (धात्री), and ashoka (अशोक)—is believed to confer spiritual benefits, aligning with practices described in the Skanda Purana [5].
