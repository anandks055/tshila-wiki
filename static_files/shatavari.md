### Overview of Sarasvatī-Sindhu Civilization [ref1]

The Sarasvatī-Sindhu Civilization (also known as the Indus Valley Civilization) is one of the earliest and most advanced urban civilizations in ancient history, dating back to around 8000 BCE with its peak during the period from 2600 BCE to 1900 BCE. This civilization thrived along the banks of the Sarasvati and Indus rivers, covering a vast territory that included parts of modern-day India, Pakistan, and Afghanistan. The ancient urban centers discovered thus far include Harappa in Punjab (Pakistan) and Mohenjo-Daro in Sindh (Pakistan), as well as Dholavira and Lothal in Gujarat (India). Notably, the oldest documented settlement from this civilization is Bhirrana in modern-day Haryana, India.

The Sarasvatī-Sindhu Civilization was characterized by advanced urban planning, standardized weights and measures, a complex economy based on agriculture and trade, and rich cultural practices including music, dance, and religious rituals. The discovery of artifacts such as seals depicting deities like Pashupati (an early form of Shiva) and the Great Bath in Mohenjo-Daro have provided insights into their spiritual and social life. However, much remains to be understood about this civilization due to the undeciphered script found on various seals and inscriptions.

### Archaeological Discoveries

Archaeologists have uncovered over 1400 sites associated with the Sarasvatī-Sindhu Civilization across India and Pakistan, along with some sites in Afghanistan. These findings reveal a sophisticated society with well-planned urban centers featuring grid-like streets, drainage systems, granaries, and public baths. The most significant excavations include Harappa (Pakistan), Mohenjo-Daro (Pakistan), Dholavira (India), and Lothal (India).

Among the artifacts found at these sites are intricately designed seals made of steatite depicting various animals, deities, and symbols. One notable seal features a figure with three faces and an antelope, believed by many scholars to represent Shiva in his Pashupati form. This seal is considered evidence of early Shaivistic worship within the civilization.

Apart from the Pashupati seal, other findings include terracotta figurines, pottery with elaborate designs, jewelry made from precious stones like lapis lazuli and carnelian, and tools and weapons made from bronze and copper. These artifacts have provided valuable insights into the daily lives, cultural practices, and technological advancements of the people who lived in this civilization.

### Economy and Trade

The Sarasvatī-Sindhu Civilization had a robust economy that was supported by both agriculture and trade. The people cultivated crops such as wheat, barley, rice, lentils, peas, sesame seeds, and cotton. They also engaged in animal husbandry, raising cattle (zebu), sheep, goats, and pigs. Evidence of fish consumption has been found at several sites, indicating that fishing was an important part of their diet.

Trade was another crucial aspect of the economy. The civilization had extensive trade networks both within its territory and with other regions such as Mesopotamia, Sumeria, Babylonia, and Egypt. They traded goods like textiles, jewelry, metal tools, and pottery for raw materials such as lapis lazuli from Afghanistan and copper from Oman.

Standardized weights and measures were used in trade transactions, which facilitated commerce across different regions. Weights made of chert, limestone, or baked clay have been found at various sites, ranging from 1 gram to several kilograms. These standard units helped ensure fair exchanges and maintain trust among traders.

### Cultural Practices and Religion

The Sarasvatī-Sindhu Civilization is known for its rich cultural practices and religious beliefs. The people practiced a form of Shaivism, as evidenced by the numerous depictions of Shiva in various forms such as Pashupati (Lord of Animals) and Ahmuvan (a standing meditative figure). The use of lingas (phallic symbols representing Shiva) was prevalent, suggesting that worship centered around these objects.

Music and dance were important aspects of their culture. Instruments like the vīṇā (lute), cymbals, mṛdaṅga (drum), and other stringed instruments have been found at various sites. These musical traditions likely played a significant role in religious ceremonies and social gatherings.

Artistic expressions are evident through terracotta figurines, sculptures, and pottery with intricate designs. Jewelry made from precious stones such as lapis lazuli and carnelian was common among the elite class. The use of these materials suggests a sophisticated level of craftsmanship and trade relations with distant regions.

### Decline and Legacy

The Sarasvatī-Sindhu Civilization began to decline around 1900 BCE, though some sites continued to be inhabited until about 1300 BCE. Several factors have been proposed for the civilization's downfall, including climate change leading to droughts and floods, ecological disturbances, natural calamities such as earthquakes, and possibly internal conflicts.

The drying up of the Sarasvati River is considered one of the main reasons for the decline. Radiocarbon dating suggests that this river dried up around 1900 BCE due to tectonic shifts and climatic changes. As a result, many settlements along its banks were abandoned as people migrated eastwards towards the Yamuna-Ganges region.

Despite its eventual demise, the Sarasvatī-Sindhu Civilization left behind a lasting legacy in terms of architectural designs, urban planning, agricultural techniques, and cultural practices that influenced subsequent civilizations in India. Some scholars argue that it represents the Vedic culture described in ancient Indian texts like the Rigveda, linking it to later Hindu traditions.

### Religious Convictions

The Sarasvatī-Sindhu Civilization had a rich spiritual life, centered around various deities and religious symbols. The most prominent deity depicted in their artifacts is Pashupati, an early form of Shiva. This figure appears on seals with three faces, an antelope, and the attitude of yoga, suggesting connections to Shaivistic worship.

In addition to Pashupati, other deities like Durga (the mother goddess) were also worshipped. The presence of lingas in many sites indicates that phallic symbols representing Shiva were a central part of their religious practices. These lingas often had three faces and were associated with the lord of animals, further emphasizing Shaivistic influences.

Animals such as bison, fish, serpents, and holy basil (tulasi) also held spiritual significance in this civilization. Figures found at Ganweriwala show people bowing before trees, indicating reverence for nature and natural elements. The use of swastikas, both clockwise and counterclockwise, on seals and walls symbolized auspiciousness and well-being.

Weapons such as bows, arrows, spears, axes, maces, catapults, and slings were used by soldiers in this civilization. Weights and measures had been standardized to facilitate trade and commerce, with one scale discovered having very accurate markings.

### Legacy

The Sarasvatī-Sindhu Civilization represents one of the earliest and most advanced urban societies in ancient history. Its achievements include sophisticated urban planning, standardized weights and measures, advanced metallurgy techniques, and rich cultural practices centered around Shaivistic worship.

Despite its eventual decline around 1900 BCE due to various factors including climate change and ecological disturbances, this civilization left behind a significant legacy that influenced subsequent cultures in India. The architectural designs, urban planning principles, agricultural techniques, and cultural practices of the Sarasvatī-Sindhu Civilization continue to be studied by scholars today as they provide valuable insights into early human settlements and societal development.

The undeciphered script found on various seals remains one of the greatest mysteries associated with this civilization, offering a window into understanding their language and communication systems. Continued research and excavation efforts at existing sites and potential new discoveries may shed further light on the intricacies of this ancient society and its profound impact on human history.
